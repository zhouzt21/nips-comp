# Empirical Bayes For the Reluctant FrequentistThanks: Version: August 24, 2026.

Roger Koenker and Jiaying Gu

###### Abstract.

Empirical Bayes methods offer valuable tools for a large class of compound decision problems. In this tutorial we describe some basic principles of the empirical Bayes paradigm stressing their frequentist interpretation. Emphasis is placed on recent developments of nonparametric maximum likelihood methods for estimating mixture models. A more extensive introductory treatment will eventually be available in [Koenker and Gu (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib42). The methods are illustrated with an extended application to models of heterogeneous income dynamics based on PSID data.

## 1. Introduction

Empirical Bayes decision theory as introduced by [Robbins (1951)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib58); [Robbins (1956)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib59) represented a challenge to both the [Wald (1950)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib67) and [Savage (1954)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib60) strands of classical decision theory. Together with the revelations of [Stein (1956)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib62) on the inadmissibility of the sample mean of a multivariate Gaussian vector in dimensions greater than two, Robbins’ results showed that compound decision problems, that is, ensembles of exchangeable decision problems could be fruitfully combined to yield improved decisions for the entire ensemble. In effect, prior information could be extracted from the ensemble yielding decision rules that performed better than classical procedures that treated each problem in isolation.

A simple example illustrating the benefit of this “borrowing of strength” from several related problems appears in [Robbins (1951)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib58). Suppose we observe independent realizations, Y1,⋯,YnY\_{1},\\cdots,Y\_{n} with each Yi∼𝒩⁡(θi,1)Y\_{i}\\sim\\mathcal{N}(\\theta\_{i},1) and θi∈{−1,1}\\theta\_{i}\\in\\{-1,1\\}. Our objective is to choose 𝜽^=(θ^1,⋯,θ^n)\\hat{\\bm{\\theta}}=(\\hat{\\theta}\_{1},\\cdots,\\hat{\\theta}\_{n}) to minimize the aggregate loss,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | L⁡(𝜽^,𝜽)=(2​n)−1​∑i=1n\|θ^i−θi\|.L(\\bm{\\hat{\\theta}},\\bm{\\theta})=(2n)^{-1}\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}\|\\hat{\\theta}\_{i}-\\theta\_{i}\|. |  |

The minimax rule for any single component of this problem is to choose θ^i=sgn​(yi)\\hat{\\theta}\_{i}=\\text{sgn}(y\_{i}). Robbins shows that this rule is also minimax for every component of the entire vector. The least favorable configuration of the θi\\theta\_{i} is one for which each component is ±1\\pm 1 with equal probability as if decided by a coin flip. This minimax rule yields an expected loss of Φ⁡(−1)≈0.159\\Phi(-1)\\approx 0.159; it is also minimax regret. Can we do any better than this? Suppose, having seen the entire sample, y1,⋯,yny\_{1},\\cdots,y\_{n}, we find that most of observations are positive. Should we really stick stubbornly with the minimax view that the θi\\theta\_{i} take values, ±1\\pm 1 with equal probability? It seems rather perverse. Suppose we knew that the probability that θ=1\\theta=1 was pp, then we could easily compute the conditional probability that θ=1\\theta=1 given any particular yy as,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | ℙp​(θ=1\|y)=p​φ​(y−1)p​φ​(y−1)+(1−p)​φ​(y+1).\\mathbb{P}\_{p}(\\theta=1\|y)=\\frac{p\\varphi(y-1)}{p\\varphi(y-1)+(1-p)\\varphi(y+1)}. |  |

Evaluating the standard Gaussian density, φ\\varphi, and simplifying we obtain the alternative decision rule,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | θ^i=sgn​(y+0.5​log⁡(p/(1−p))).\\hat{\\theta}\_{i}=\\text{sgn}(y+0.5\\log(p/(1-p))). |  |

Robbins suggests that we might *estimate* the probability pp by p^=(y¯+1)/2\\hat{p}=(\\bar{y}+1)/2, and consider a plug-in version of this revised decision rule. The logistic adjustment has the desirable effect of biasing our estimates toward the more likely of the two alternatives, for example if p^=3/4\\hat{p}=3/4 we would use θ^i=sgn​(yi+.549)\\hat{\\theta}\_{i}=\\text{sgn}(y\_{i}+.549), reflecting our evidence that Yi=1Y\_{i}=1 was more likely than its alternative. This is an empirical Bayes procedure. There has been no reliance on subjective prior information, nor on the pessimistic principle of minimaxity, only the empirical evidence of the observed sample has been relied upon. Robbins’ method of moments estimator of pp, could be replaced by other approaches such as maximum likelihood or even a formal conjugate Bayes procedure with a Beta prior, but ignoring the information contained in the ensemble of problems can be extremely costly in this setting. With our hypothetical p=3/4p=3/4 the asymptotic risk of using p^\\hat{p} is reduced by about 20 percent. This is a leading example of Robbins’ claim for the “asymptotic sub-minimaxity” of empirical Bayes procedures relative to the minimax procedures of Wald.

For the Bayesophobic the foregoing example should raise no anxieties, they need only consider how to go about constructing a reasonable estimate of the probability pp. Likewise, Bayesians should be entirely comfortable computing a full posterior for pp that could be used to inform their decisions about the θi\\theta\_{i}’s. More complicated compound decision problems will require more complicated estimation of the mixture structure of the problem, and therein lies the charm of the empirical Bayes approach. Bayesian language and the machinery of Bayesian computation will prove to be convenient, but no further commitment to the catechism of the Reverend Bayes is required. Indeed, we will argue that empirical Bayes methodology represents an alloy of the best features of the frequentist and Bayesian traditions.

## 2. The Compound Decision Paradigm

Suppose that we are faced with an exchangeable, i.e. permutation invariant, ensemble of related problems:

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | Yi∼φ(y\|θi),i=1,2,⋯,n,Y\_{i}\\sim\\varphi(y\|\\theta\_{i}),\\quad i=1,2,\\cdots,n, |  |

where φ\\varphi denotes some familiar – usually exponential family – density, indexed by parameters, θi\\theta\_{i}, that express the underlying heterogeneity of the problems. Our task is to choose a decision rule, 𝜹:𝒴→𝒜\\bm{\\delta}:\\mathcal{Y}\\to\\mathcal{A}, mapping realizations Y=(Y1,⋯,Yn)Y=(Y\_{1},\\cdots,Y\_{n}) in the sample space, 𝒴\\mathcal{Y}, to actions, a=δ⁡(Y)a=\\delta(Y), in an action space 𝒜\\mathcal{A} that minimize the compound risk,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | Rn​(𝜹,𝜽)=n−1​𝔼​∑i=1nL⁡(𝜹i​(Y),θi).R\_{n}(\\bm{\\delta},\\bm{\\theta})=n^{-1}\\mathbb{E}\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}L(\\bm{\\delta}\_{i}(Y),\\theta\_{i}). |  |

Following [Robbins (1956)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib59) we will restrict attention to simple, symmetric decision rules for which 𝜹i​(y1,⋯,yn)=δ⁡(yi)\\bm{\\delta}\_{i}(y\_{1},\\cdots,y\_{n})=\\delta(y\_{i}). Such rules are now often referred to as separable rules. The function, δ\\delta may depend upon the entire sample, but given our objective and the exchangeable probabilistic structure of the problems there seems to be little merit in venturing beyond this class. With this restriction we can write,

|  |  |  |  |
|---|---|---|---|
|  | Rn​(δ,𝜽)\\displaystyle R\_{n}(\\delta,\\bm{\\theta}) | =n−1​𝔼​∑i=1nL⁡(δ⁡(Yi),θi)\\displaystyle=n^{-1}\\mathbb{E}\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}L(\\delta(Y\_{i}),\\theta\_{i}) |  |
|  |  | =n−1​∑i=1n∫L⁡(δ⁡(y),θi)​φ​(y\|θi)​𝑑y\\displaystyle=n^{-1}\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}\\int L(\\delta(y),\\theta\_{i})\\varphi(y\|\\theta\_{i})dy |  |
|  |  | =∫∫⁡L⁡(δ⁡(y),θ)​φ​(y\|θ)​d​Gn​(θ)​𝑑y\\displaystyle=\\int\\int L(\\delta(y),\\theta)\\varphi(y\|\\theta)dG\_{n}(\\theta)dy |  |

where Gn​(θ)=n−1​∑i=1n𝟙​(θi≤θ)G\_{n}(\\theta)=n^{-1}\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}\\mathbbm{1}(\\theta\_{i}\\leq\\theta) denotes the empirical distribution function of the θi\\theta\_{i}’s. This is obviously an oracle risk function since we do not know GnG\_{n}, but it provides a natural benchmark for evaluating the performance of various feasible decision rules.

How related do the problems need to be? [Robbins (1951)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib58) infamously opined,

> No relation whatever is assumed to hold among the unknown parameters θi\\theta\_{i}. To emphasize this point, Y1Y\_{1} could be an observation on a butterfly in Ecuador, Y2Y\_{2} on an oyster in Maryland, Y3Y\_{3} the temperature of a star, and so on, all observations being taken at different times.

Of course, this is a bit disingenuous since we have already asserted that observations share a common conditional density, and that they are exchangeable. Ultimately, this issue of relatedness, or what [Efron (2010)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib16) refers to as relevance, is bound up with the form of the mixing distribution GnG\_{n}.

###### Theorem 1 (Fundamental Theorem of Compound Decisions).

For separable decision rules compound risk is equal to the Bayes risk of a single copy of the compound decision problem with respect to the “prior” GnG\_{n}.

Thus, an optimal decision rule for our compound decision problem can be expressed as a Bayes rule minimizing posterior loss with respect to the “prior” GnG\_{n}:

|  |  |  |  |
|---|---|---|---|
|  | Bn​(δ)\\displaystyle B\_{n}(\\delta) | =∫{∫ΘL⁡(δ⁡(y),θ)​φ​(y\|θ)​d​Gn​(θ)}​𝑑y.\\displaystyle=\\int\\left\\{\\int\_{\\Theta}L(\\delta(y),\\theta)\\varphi(y\|\\theta)dG\_{n}(\\theta)\\right\\}dy. |  |
|  |  | =∫{∫ΘL⁡(δ⁡(y),θ)​h​(θ\|y)​𝑑θ}​f​(y)​𝑑y.\\displaystyle=\\int\\left\\{\\int\_{\\Theta}L(\\delta(y),\\theta)h(\\theta\|y)d\\theta\\right\\}f(y)dy. |  |

where h⁡(θ|y)=φ⁡(y|θ)​d​Gn​(θ)/f⁡(y)h(\\theta|y)=\\varphi(y|\\theta)dG\_{n}(\\theta)/f(y), is the posterior distribution of θ\\theta given Y=yY=y, and f⁡(y)=∫φ⁡(y|θ)​d​Gn​(θ)f(y)=\\int\\varphi(y|\\theta)dG\_{n}(\\theta) is the marginal density of the YiY\_{i}.

From a formal Bayesian perspective exchangeability of the YiY\_{i}’s yields via de Finetti’s theorem a mixture representation of the Bayes risk with a generic prior, GG, replacing the frequentist, GnG\_{n}. At this point the two warring perspectives are essentially indistinguishable; controversy arises only when we begin to ask where can we find a viable GG that will enable us to make good decisions. In the absence of a priori knowledge of GG, this apparently requires some way to estimate the empirical distribution function GnG\_{n} of the latent parameters θi\\theta\_{i}. In our introductory example with θi∈{−1,1}\\theta\_{i}\\in\\{-1,1\\} this entailed estimating a scalar probability, more general settings demand more. We will consider parametric models for GnG\_{n} in the next section, and then proceed to consider nonparamtric methods in the following section. Robbins with characteristic wit referred to this task as “Estimating the Inestimable” in a lecture series presented at Berkeley in the late 1980s.

## 3. Parametric Priors

When in doubt about the form of an unknown distribution, the Gaussian shape of Napolean’s hat naturally springs to mind. Suppose, instead of restricting the θi\\theta\_{i}’s to take only the values ±1\\pm 1, we assume instead that they are Gaussian with mean, zero, and variance, σ02\\sigma\_{0}^{2}, i.e. θi∼𝒩⁡(0,σ02)\\theta\_{i}\\sim\\mathcal{N}(0,\\sigma\_{0}^{2}), and retain the assumption that they are embedded in standard Gaussian noise. It follows that the marginal distribution of the YiY\_{i}’s is Gaussian with mean, zero, and variance, 1+σ021+\\sigma\_{0}^{2}. Under quadratic loss, L⁡(δ⁡(y),θ)=(δ⁡(y)−θ)2L(\\delta(y),\\theta)=(\\delta(y)-\\theta)^{2}, the posterior mean,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | δ⁡(y)=𝔼⁡(θ\|Y=y)=(1−11+σ02)​y,\\delta(y)=\\mathbb{E}(\\theta\|Y=y)=\\left(1-\\frac{1}{1+\\sigma\_{0}^{2}}\\right)y, |  |

is the optimal Bayes rule, provided that σ02\\sigma\_{0}^{2} is known. If not, we can rely instead on an estimate based on S≡∑i=1nYi2∼(1+σ02)​χn2S\\equiv\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}Y\_{i}^{2}\\sim(1+\\sigma\_{0}^{2})\\chi\_{n}^{2}. Recalling that an inverse χn2\\chi\_{n}^{2} random variable has expectation, (n−2)−1(n-2)^{-1}, we obtain the method of moments, empirical Bayes estimator,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | δ^​(y)=(1−n−2S)​y.\\hat{\\delta}(y)=\\left(1-\\frac{n-2}{S}\\right)y. |  |

This is the classical James-Stein estimator, [James and Stein (1961)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib37). It has strictly smaller compound risk than the inadmissible maximum likelihood estimator, δ¯​(y)=y\\bar{\\delta}(y)=y when n≥3n\\geq 3 for any sequence of θi\\theta\_{i}’s. Linear shrinkage of each coordinate toward zero may not result in a more accurate estimate for any one coordinate, but the compound risk of the entire vector is reduced. See the Appendix for a formal exposition.

There are many variations on the basic James-Stein rule: if we allow the Gaussian prior to have (unknown) mean, θ0\\theta\_{0}, estimable by the sample mean, Y¯n=n−1​∑i=1nYi\\bar{Y}\_{n}=n^{-1}\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}Y\_{i}, we obtain the Efron-Morris rule,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | δ\~​(y)=Y¯n+(1−n−3S\~)​(y−Y¯n),\\tilde{\\delta}(y)=\\bar{Y}\_{n}+\\left(1-\\frac{n-3}{\\tilde{S}}\\right)(y-\\bar{Y}\_{n}), |  |

with S\~≡∑i=1n(Yi−Y¯n)2\\tilde{S}\\equiv\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}(Y\_{i}-\\bar{Y}\_{n})^{2}, so shrinkage occurs toward the sample mean rather than zero. It can happen that S\~&lt;n−3\\tilde{S}&lt;n-3 in which case the shrinkage factor would flip the signs of the coordinate effects. This motivates consideration of “positive part” variants of the foregoing rules that restrict shrinkage to be non-negative.

In the figure, the solid line represents an oracle version of the Stein rule, while the dashed line is an estimate thereof; both shrink observed yiy\_{i}’s toward their collective sample mean in an effort to replace the naive maximum likelihood estimates, δ⁡(yi)=yi\\delta(y\_{i})=y\_{i} (on the 45 degree line) with a more precise estimate of δ⁡(y)=𝔼⁡(θ|Y=y)\\delta(y)=\\mathbb{E}(\\theta|Y=y).

Stein’s revelation that the maximum likelihood estimator for the mean of a multivariate Gaussian random vector was inadmissible under quadratic loss in dimensions greater than two came as a surprise to many practicing statisticians and is still perhaps not fully appreciated in the econometrics literature.

Stigler’s “Galtonian perspective on shrinkage estimators,” [Stigler (1990)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib63), offers a novel interpretation of Stein shrinkage that may aid intuition. Imagine a thought experiment in which we observe not only the YiY\_{i}’s, but also their associated θi\\theta\_{i}’s, which we can then plot as the points appearing in Figure [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#S3.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 3. Parametric Priors ‣ Empirical BayesFor the Reluctant Frequentist"). We are interested in the conditional expectation, 𝔼⁡(θ|Y=y)\\mathbb{E}(\\theta|Y=y), which is nothing but the regression line in this figure. Of course we don’t have access to the actual θi\\theta\_{i}’s, so this might seem a bit fanciful, but the coefficients of the regression line require only the mean of the θi\\theta\_{i}’s and the ℂ​ov⁡(Y,θ)\\operatorname{\\mathbb{C}ov}(Y,\\theta), which can be consistently estimated by Y¯n\\bar{Y}\_{n} and S\~/(n−2)−1\\tilde{S}/(n-2)-1, respectively. In the Figure the population regression line is depicted as the dashed red line, while the sample regression is dotted and blue. The grey 45 degree line contrasts the unshrunken, δ⁡(y)=y\\delta(y)=y estimator with the two variants of the Stein rule.

Further elaboration of the idea of modeling the prior GG as Gaussian may incorporate heterogeneity in the precision of the observed YiY\_{i}’s. Consider the model,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | yi​j∼𝒩(μi,σi2),i=1,…,n,j=1,…,J.y\_{ij}\\sim\\mathcal{N}(\\mu\_{i},\\sigma\_{i}^{2}),\\quad i=1,\\dots,n,\\;j=1,\\dots,J. |  |

The investigator observes pairs, (μ^i,σ^i2)(\\hat{\\mu}\_{i},\\hat{\\sigma}\_{i}^{2}) with μ^i=J−1​∑jyi​j\\hat{\\mu}\_{i}=J^{-1}\\sum\_{j}y\_{ij} and σ^i2=(J−1)−1​∑j(yi​j−μ^i)2\\hat{\\sigma}\_{i}^{2}=(J-1)^{-1}\\sum\_{j}(y\_{ij}-\\hat{\\mu}\_{i})^{2}; the objective is often to test the hypotheses: H0:μi=0H\_{0}:\\mu\_{i}=0, or to select a subset that violate these hypotheses. In genomics this is often accomplished with a parametric empirical Bayes procedure implemented in the R package limma. Rather than positing a prior on the full parameter space, limma assumes that the σi2\\sigma\_{i}^{2} are independent of the μi\\mu\_{i} and are drawn iidly from the inverse chi-squared distribution,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | σi−2∼(v0​s02)−1​χv02i=1,…,n.\\sigma\_{i}^{-2}\\sim(v\_{0}s\_{0}^{2})^{-1}\\chi\_{v\_{0}}^{2}\\quad i=1,\\dots,n. |  |

The hyperparameters, v0v\_{0} and s0s\_{0} can be estimated by maximum likelihood. The null hypotheses H0:μi=0H\_{0}:\\mu\_{i}=0 have pp-values,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | pi=2​(1−Ft,v0+v​(\|t\~i\|)),p\_{i}=2(1-F\_{t,v\_{0}+v}(\|\\tilde{t}\_{i}\|)), |  |

where t\~i=μ^i/s\~i\\tilde{t}\_{i}=\\hat{\\mu}\_{i}/\\tilde{s}\_{i}, s\~i2=(v0​s02+v​σ^i2)/(v0+v)\\tilde{s}\_{i}^{2}=(v\_{0}s\_{0}^{2}+v\\hat{\\sigma}\_{i}^{2})/(v\_{0}+v), v=J−1v=J-1 and Ft,vF\_{t,v} is the distribution function of a Student tt random variable with vv degrees of freedom. The parametric prior for the σi2\\sigma\_{i}^{2} shrinks the σ^i2\\hat{\\sigma}\_{i}^{2} toward a common value and is intended to improve precision. The imposition of prior information on nuisance parameters while leaving the parameters of primary interest, in this case the μi\\mu\_{i}, alone is referred to as “partially Bayes” by [Cox (1975)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib13). See [Ignatiadis and Sen (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib36). More flexible nonparametric procedures will be considered in the next section.

[Lindley and Smith (1972)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib48), appealing to results of [de Finetti (1964)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib21) greatly elaborated the paradigm of Gaussian linear models with parametric Gaussian priors, and thus initiated the modern development of hierarchical models. They start from the compound decision premise that observations arise in an exchangeable fashion, so the YiY\_{i}’s come from a mixture density. When both the conditional density, φ⁡(y|θ)\\varphi(y\\;|\\;\\theta), and the mixing distribution, GG, are Gaussian, this leads to some elegant linear algebra.

###### Proposition 1 (Lindley and Smith).

Suppose, for θ1∈ℝp1\\theta\_{1}\\in\\mathbb{R}^{p\_{1}}, our observed response y∈ℝny\\in\\mathbb{R}^{n} is multivariate Gaussian with mean vector A1​θ1A\_{1}\\theta\_{1} and covariance matrix C1C\_{1}, i.e. y∼𝒩⁡(A1​θ1,C1)y\\sim\\mathcal{N}(A\_{1}\\theta\_{1},C\_{1}). Then, if θ1\\theta\_{1} is also Gaussian, θ1∼𝒩⁡(A2​θ2,C2)\\theta\_{1}\\sim\\mathcal{N}(A\_{2}\\theta\_{2},C\_{2}), the marginal distribution of yy is 𝒩⁡(A1​A2​θ2,C1+A1​C2​A1⊤)\\mathcal{N}(A\_{1}A\_{2}\\theta\_{2},C\_{1}+A\_{1}C\_{2}A\_{1}^{\\top}) and θ1|Y∼𝒩⁡(B​b,B)\\theta\_{1}|Y\\sim\\mathcal{N}(Bb,B) where B−1=A1⊤​C1−1​A1+C2−1B^{-1}=A\_{1}^{\\top}C\_{1}^{-1}A\_{1}+C\_{2}^{-1}, and b=A1⊤​C1−1​y+C2−1​A2​θ2b=A\_{1}^{\\top}C\_{1}^{-1}y+C\_{2}^{-1}A\_{2}\\theta\_{2}.

The linear model structure makes these shrinkage formulae more complicated, but it is still possible to recognize that the posterior mean of θ1\\theta\_{1} is a matrix weighted average of the response vector, yy, and the prior mean θ2\\theta\_{2}. The closely related papers of [Chamberlain and Leamer (1976)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib11); [Leamer and Chamberlain (1976)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib47) offer further insight into this phenomenon. Estimation of such hierarchical models generally requires some form of MCMC procedure, although when the covariance matrices, C1C\_{1} and C2C\_{2} are spherical the formulae reduce to classical variance component analysis that goes back to work by [Balestra and Nerlove (1966)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib4) on the demand for natural gas in the econometrics literature.

Parametric non-Gaussian priors for linear regression models have emerged as a familiar device in modern high dimensional statistical modeling. When we consider penalized regression estimators that optimize,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | min∑i=1n(yi−xiβ)2+λP(β),\\min\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}(y\_{i}-x\_{i}\\beta)^{2}+\\lambda P(\\beta), |  |

we have available a large menu of choices for the penalty function PP. The lasso penalty, P⁡(β)=‖β‖1P(\\beta)=\\|\\beta\\|\_{1}, employing the ℓ1\\ell\_{1}-norm, is most familiar once we depart from the Gaussian “ridge” penalty, P⁡(β)=‖β‖22P(\\beta)=\\|\\beta\\|\_{2}^{2}. It corresponds to imposing independent Laplace priors on β\\beta. Choice of the tuning parameter, λ\\lambda controls how strongly we believe in the prior. As soon as we choose λ\\lambda by cross-validation or some other form of sorcery we have ventured into the realm of empirical Bayes.

Another prominent option for the penalty, PP, is treat the coordinates of β\\beta as if they were drawn iidly from the Cauchy distribution, as considered by [Johnstone and Silverman (2004)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib39) and [Castillo and van der Vaart (2012)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib9). Although such priors are generally structured to shrink coefficients toward zero, this is typically rationalized by some form of prior standardization of the design matrix. The empirical aspect of these procedures is generally restricted to choice of the tuning parameter λ\\lambda representing the scale of the prior density. However, more flexibility can be achieved by permitting larger parametric families, for example [Azevedo et al (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib3) a class of Student priors for large-scale A/B testing settings with location, scale and degrees of freedom of the prior estimated by maximum likelihood.

Returning to the simple Gaussian sequence model with scalar parameeters θi\\theta\_{i}, in Figure [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ 3. Parametric Priors ‣ Empirical BayesFor the Reluctant Frequentist") we contrast several forms of shrinkage: linear shrinkage with the classical Stein rule, the lasso procedure that shrinks moderately when yy is near zero, and the Cauchy penalty that shrinks very aggressively near zero while large departures from zero are shrunken very little. It should be stressed that tuning the location and scale of these penalties offers some flexibility, but the selection of a functional form for such parametric priors involves a leap of Bayesian faith that may trouble some researchers.

Thus far we have focused entirely on settings in which our base model, φ⁡(y|θ)\\varphi(y|\\theta) is Gaussian. Parametric mixture priors play an important role in many other corners of statistics. Poisson models are often paired with gamma mixing, and the modern literature on survival analysis, is permeated by parametric models of “frailty.” As anticipated by the pioneering critique of [Heckman and Singer (1984)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib32), choosing a specific parametric model for frailty can be difficult so it is natural to turn to nonparametric methods for guidance.

## 4. Nonparametric Prior Estimation

Parametric models for the prior, GG, can be difficult to choose, so it is natural to ask whether some nonparametric procedure can be used to estimate GG. An affirmative answer to this question can be traced back to an abstract of [Robbins (1950)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib57), much more fully elaborated in [Kiefer and Wolfowitz (1956)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib40). Some further development of the idea of nonparametric maximum likelihood estimation of mixture models was made by [Pfanzagl (1988)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib54), but practical implementation of these methods was delayed until [Laird (1978)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib46) showed how the nascent EM algorithm, [Dempster et al (1977)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib15), could be used to compute it. [Heckman and Singer (1984)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib32) pioneered the EM approach to explore the sensitivity to various parametric frailty models of duration models in econometrics. [Lindsay (1981)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib49); [Lindsay (1995)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib50) further clarified many aspects of the NPMLE, but computation remained a bottleneck due to slow convergence of the EM algorithm. Fortunately, modern developments in convex optimization have substantially improved computational prospects for the NPMLE.

It is easy to see that the NPMLE problem for mixtures is convex. We have a strictly convex objective subject to linear constraints over the convex set, 𝒢\\mathcal{G}, of distribution functions:

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | minG∈𝒢{−∑i=1nlogf(yi)\|f(yi)=∫φ(yi\|θ)dG(θ),i=1,⋯,n}\\min\_{G\\in\\mathcal{G}}\\left\\{-\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}\\log f(y\_{i})\\;\|\\;f(y\_{i})=\\int\\varphi(y\_{i}\|\\theta)dG(\\theta),\\;i=1,\\cdots,n\\right\\} |  |

The problem is infinite dimensional, but it can be solved to desirable precision by restricting the support of solutions to a finite dimensional grid (t1&lt;t2&lt;⋯&lt;tm)(t\_{1}&lt;t\_{2}&lt;\\cdots&lt;t\_{m}) contained by the empirical support of the observations. Finite dimensional formulations can be expressed given this restriction. In primal form with AA denoting an nn by mm matrix with typical element φ⁡(yi|tj)\\varphi(y\_{i}|t\_{j}), and 𝒈∈𝒮m\\bm{g}\\in\\mathcal{S}\_{m} denoting the masses associated with each of the grid points and 𝒮m\\mathcal{S}\_{m} the mm-dimensional unit simplex, a primal formulation is given by:

|  |  |  |  |
|---|---|---|---|
| (P) |  | min𝒈∈𝒮m{−∑i=1nlogfi\|𝒇=A𝒈,}\\min\_{\\bm{g}\\in\\mathcal{S}\_{m}}\\left\\{-\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}\\log f\_{i}\\;\|\\;\\bm{f}=A\\bm{g},\\;\\right\\} |  |

and in dual form as:

|  |  |  |  |
|---|---|---|---|
| (D) |  | max𝝂∈ℝn⁡{∑i=1nlog⁡νi\|A⊤​𝝂≤n​𝟙m}.\\max\_{\\bm{\\nu}\\in\\mathbb{R}^{n}}\\left\\{\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}\\log\\nu\_{i}\\;\|\\;A^{\\top}\\bm{\\nu}\\leq n\\mathbbm{1}\_{m}\\;\\right\\}. |  |

The dual form is usually somewhat more convenient for computation, and the primal solution can be easily recovered from the dual solution by solving for the coordinates of 𝒈\\bm{g} from the linear system,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | ∑jφ⁡(yi\|tj)​gj=1ν^i,\\sum\_{j}\\varphi(y\_{i}\|t\_{j})g\_{j}=\\frac{1}{\\hat{\\nu}\_{i}}, |  |

restricted to the set {i:ν^i&gt;0}\\{i:\\hat{\\nu}\_{i}&gt;0\\}, that is to those observations whose dual constraint is active at the dual solution. The number of these active constraints, m∗m^{\*}, is typically far fewer than its obvious upper bound of nn, indeed it has been recently shown by [Polyanskiy and Wu (2020)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib55) that as n→∞n\\to\\infty, m∗=𝒪⁡(log⁡n)m^{\*}=\\mathcal{O}(\\log n) when GG has sub-Gaussian tails. This self-regularizing feature of the NPMLE may come as a surprise since many infinite dimensional inverse problems are ill-posed and do require some form of regularization, and consequent tuning parameter selection. In contrast, the NPMLE determines of the number, location, and mass of the atoms of the G^\\hat{G} solution from the data without any interference by the analyst.

Identifiability of GG in mixture models is thoroughly treated by [Teicher (1961)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib64); [Teicher (1967)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib65) for a scalar mixing parameter.

###### Definition 1.

Let Φ⁡(y|θ)\\Phi(y|\\theta) be a distribution function defined for all θ∈Θ⊂ℝ\\theta\\in\\Theta\\subset\\mathbb{R} and GG be a distribution function defined on Θ\\Theta, the mixture,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | F⁡(y)=∫ΘΦ⁡(y\|θ)​𝑑G​(θ)F(y)=\\int\_{\\Theta}\\Phi(y\|\\theta)dG(\\theta) |  |

is identifiable if and only if there is a unique GG yielding FF.

For location, Φ⁡(y−θ)\\Phi(y-\\theta), and scale, Φ⁡(y​θ)\\Phi(y\\theta), mixtures this follows from the uniqueness of the characteristic function provided that the Fourier transforms of Φ⁡(y)\\Phi(y) and Φ⁡(ey)\\Phi(e^{y}) respectively are not identically zero on some non-degenerate real interval, a condition that is trivially satisfied in most of the conventional empirical Bayes settings in which φ⁡(y|θ)\\varphi(y|\\theta) is a continuous (Lebesgue) density. In multivariate settings and many discrete data settings identification conditions are more delicate and partial identification is not uncommon. See [Koenker and Gu (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib42) for further details and references.

Given an estimate, G^\\hat{G}, what should we do with it? Minimizing posterior compound loss is simplest with quadratic loss since it requires only computing posterior means. This is particularly convenient when φ\\varphi is of the exponential family form.

###### Proposition 2.

(Tweedie) For φ\\varphi of the (natural) exponential family form,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | φ⁡(y\|η)=m⁡(y)​ey​η​h​(η),\\varphi(y\|\\eta)=m(y)e^{y\\eta}h(\\eta), |  |

the posterior mean is,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | δ⁡(y)≡𝔼⁡(η\|Y=y)=dd​y​log⁡(fG​(y)/m⁡(y)),\\delta(y)\\equiv\\mathbb{E}(\\eta\|Y=y)=\\frac{d}{dy}\\log(f\_{G}(y)/m(y)), |  |

where fG​(y)=∫φ⁡(y|η)​𝑑G​(η)f\_{G}(y)=\\int\\varphi(y|\\eta)dG(\\eta), is the marginal distribution of YY. And δ⁡(y)\\delta(y) is non-decreasing in yy.

See Appendix [A](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#A1 "Appendix A Tweedie’s Formula ‣ Empirical BayesFor the Reluctant Frequentist") for a proof and some further details. When φ\\varphi is standard Gaussian, so m(y)=e−y2/2m(y)=e^{-y^{2}/2}, the posterior mean becomes,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | δ⁡(y)≡𝔼⁡(η\|Y=y)=y+fG′​(y)/fG​(y).\\delta(y)\\equiv\\mathbb{E}(\\eta\|Y=y)=y+f\_{G}^{\\prime}(y)/f\_{G}(y). |  |

In this Gaussian case, fG′​(y)/fG​(y)f\_{G}^{\\prime}(y)/f\_{G}(y) can be interpreted as a shrinkage term that pulls the naive estimator, η^=y\\hat{\\eta}=y to its posterior mean.

It is tempting to interpret the Tweedie formula as an invitation to estimate the marginal density fGf\_{G} and construct estimates of the posterior mean accordingly. In the terminology of [Efron (2014)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib17), this would be ff-modeling in contrast to gg-modeling which relies on a preliminary estimator of GG. Although estimation of fGf\_{G} by conventional kernel methods is easy, estimation of its log derivative is more challenging. More seriously, when φ\\varphi is of the exponential family form Proposition [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2. ‣ 4. Nonparametric Prior Estimation ‣ Empirical BayesFor the Reluctant Frequentist") asserts that the posterior mean function is monotone in yy, a condition that is awkward to impose for standard density estimators. See [Koenker and Mizera (2014)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib44) for a proposal for a shape constrained nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator of fGf\_{G} that imposes such a monotonicity constraint.

###### Example 1.

To illustrate the NPMLE in a simple Gaussian location mixture setting, suppose that we observe a random sample of size n=1000n=1000, with Yi∼𝒩⁡(θi,1)Y\_{i}\\sim\\mathcal{N}(\\theta\_{i},1) and (θi−3)(\\theta\_{i}-3) distributed as standard lognormal. The left panel of Figure [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4. Nonparametric Prior Estimation ‣ Empirical BayesFor the Reluctant Frequentist") shows a histogram of the observed, YiY\_{i}’s, and superimposed in red are the mass points of the estimated, G^\\hat{G}. Of 1000 potential grid point locations for the illustrated NPMLE solution only 13 have associated mass in excess of 0.001. The general shape of the discrete mass points does mimic the shape of the lognormal density that was used to generate the sample yiy\_{i}’s. Viewed as a density estimate this G^\\hat{G} is quite terrible, as an estimate of a lognormal distribution function it is somewhat better. But from a practical decision making perspective, G^\\hat{G} is useful primarily as a device for evaluation of smooth linear functionals like conditional means, and for this it is quite excellent as noted in the Appendix.

The right panel of Figure [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#S4.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 4. Nonparametric Prior Estimation ‣ Empirical BayesFor the Reluctant Frequentist") plots posterior mean functions for three choices of G^\\hat{G}. The red line represents the Stein rule. The blue curve depicts the oracle rule based on full knowledge of the lognormal form of GG. And the black curve shows the estimated posterior mean corresponding to the NPMLE of GG depicted in the left panel of the figure. The linear Stein rule clearly shrinks too much in both tails. The NPMLE estimate sticks quite closely to the oracle estimate except in the far right tail where data is sparse as indicated by the “rug plot” along the horizontal axis, and its discrete form inevitably produces some oscillation around the oracle estimate.

Although the posterior mean function of the preceding example is necessarily smoothed by the convolution producing fGf\_{G}, and thus its log derivative, there are good reasons to consider smoother estimates of GG itself. This is especially apparent once we begin to consider inference for empirical Bayes point estimates or the ranking and selection problems considered in [Gu and Koenker (2023)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib28). A smoother alternative to the Kiefer-Wolfowitz NPMLE of GG, proposed by [Efron (2016)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib18), expresses the log density of GG in the exponential family form,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | log⁡g⁡(θ\|α)=∑k=1pzk⊤​(θ)​αk−γ⁡(α)\\log g(\\theta\|\\alpha)=\\sum\_{k=1}^{p}z\_{k}^{\\top}(\\theta)\\alpha\_{k}-\\gamma(\\alpha) |  |

where zk(θ)∈ℝp,k=1,⋯,pz\_{k}(\\theta)\\in\\mathbb{R}^{p},\\;k=1,\\cdots,p are elements of a natural spline basis expansion, and γ\\gamma is the usual constant of integration for the one-parameter exponential family density. The penalized Efron log likelihood is then,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | ℓ⁡(α)=∑i=1nlog⁡fα​(yi)+c0​‖α‖,\\ell(\\alpha)=\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}\\log f\_{\\alpha}(y\_{i})+c\_{0}\\\|\\alpha\\\|, |  |

where the nn vector fα=(fα​(y1),⋯,fα​(yn))f\_{\\alpha}=(f\_{\\alpha}(y\_{1}),\\cdots,f\_{\\alpha}(y\_{n})) can be written as, fα=A​Z​αf\_{\\alpha}=AZ\\alpha with ZZ an mm by pp matrix with typical element Zj​k=zk​(tj)Z\_{jk}=z\_{k}(t\_{j}). Choice of the basis ZZ, its dimension pp, and the penalty parameter c0c\_{0} all call for some degree of expert judgement that could be guided by the relatively automatic NPMLE. Letting p→∞p\\to\\infty and c0→0c\_{0}\\to 0 recovers in the limit the NPMLE, although algorithms that fail to exploit convexity may struggle with the optimization. An alternative way to achieve smoothness of an estimator of GG is simply to convolve the NPMLE G^\\hat{G} with some smooth density. This obviously would involve choosing a kernel and bandwidth, choices that might be usefully informed by a careful examination of the discrete form of the NPMLE.

When the latent parameter θ\\theta is of dimension two interior point methods for computing the NPMLE are still feasible using gridding as illustrated in [Gu and Koenker (2017b)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib26); [Gu and Koenker (2017a)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib25). However, beyond dimension two such methods become unwieldy and alternative first-order methods are probabily required. Recent progress in this direction can be found in [Soloff et al (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib61), [Zhang et al (2022)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib68).

## 5. Empirical Bayes Methods for Discrete Data

The range of empirical Bayes methods extends far beyond the Gaussian mixture settings that we have emphasized thus far. Parametric Poisson mixture models have a long history in actuarial risk analysis and ecology. See, for example [Bühlmann and Straub (1970)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib8) and [Fisher et al (1943)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib22), respectively. Given observations y1,⋯,yny\_{1},\\cdots,y\_{n} with marginal density,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | fG​(y)=∫φ⁡(y\|θ)​𝑑G​(θ),f\_{G}(y)=\\int\\varphi(y\|\\theta)dG(\\theta), |  |

where ℙ⁡(Yi=y|λ)=φ⁡(y|λi)=e−λi​λiy/y!\\mathbb{P}(Y\_{i}=y|\\lambda)=\\varphi(y|\\lambda\_{i})=e^{-\\lambda\_{i}}\\lambda\_{i}^{y}/y!, [Robbins (1956)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib59) proposed a nonparametric estimator of the posterior mean 𝔼⁡\[θ|Yi=y]\\mathbb{E}\[\\theta|Y\_{i}=y],

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | δ⁡(y)=∫θ​φ​(y\|θ)​𝑑G​(θ)∫φ⁡(y\|θ)​𝑑G​(θ)=(y+1)​fG​(y+1)fG​(y).\\delta(y)=\\frac{\\int\\theta\\varphi(y\|\\theta)dG(\\theta)}{\\int\\varphi(y\|\\theta)dG(\\theta)}=\\frac{(y+1)f\_{G}(y+1)}{f\_{G}(y)}. |  |

Since the quantities fG​(y)f\_{G}(y) can be easily estimated by the observed frequencies this is an extremely convenient ff-modeling strategy. However, like other ff-modeling methods it has the disadvantage that it may fail to respect the monotonicity of the Bayes rule as proscribed by Proposition [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#Thmproposition2 "Proposition 2. ‣ 4. Nonparametric Prior Estimation ‣ Empirical BayesFor the Reluctant Frequentist"). This is particularly problematic when GG is heavy tailed since the tail frequencies of the mixture can be highly variable. A preferable GG-modeling strategy is to employ the NPMLE, G^\\hat{G}, for GG in the Tweedie formula for the posterior mean. Although [Polyanskiy and Wu (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib56) have recently shown that the original Robbins estimator achieves a sharp asymptotic regret bound, the NPMLE GG-modeling approach exhibits significantly improved performance in simulations reported in [Koenker and Gu (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib42).

Binary response data give rise to a wide variety of mixture models that can be analysed with empirical Bayes methods. In the simplest case, suppose that we have a sample y1,⋯,yny\_{1},\\cdots,y\_{n} of outcomes from binomial experiments B⁡(m,pi)B(m,p\_{i}) as in the tack-flipping experiment of [Beckett and Diaconis (1994)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib5), who describe the protocol of the experiment as follows.

> The example involves repeated rolls of a common thumbtack. A one was recorded if the tack landed point up and a zero was recorded if the tack landed point down. All tacks started point down. Each tack was flicked or hit with the fingers from where it last rested. A fixed tack was flicked 9 times. The data are recorded in Table 1. There are 320 9-tuples. These arose from 16 different tacks, 2 “flickers,” and 10 surfaces. The tacks vary considerably in shape and in proportion of ones. The surfaces varied from rugs through tablecloths through bathroom floors.

Unconditionally on the type of tack and surface the experimental outcomes have marginal mixture density.

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | fG​(y)=ℙ⁡(Y=y)=∫(my)​py​(1−p)m−y​𝑑G​(p).f\_{G}(y)=\\mathbb{P}(Y=y)=\\int\\binom{m}{y}p^{y}(1-p)^{m-y}dG(p). |  |

Again, the mixing distribution GG can be estimated by maximum likelihood. This yields a 3-point mixture for G^\\hat{G} that can be reproduced by running demo(Bmix1) in R from the package REBayes. This solution is essentially identical to that reported by [Liu (1996)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib51) using the EM algorithm although interior point convex optimization methods are considerably quicker.

The binomial mixture model is easily adapted to situations with varying numbers of trials mm, but a cautionary note is required regarding identification in such models. Only m+1m+1 distinct frequencies can be observed for B⁡(m,p)B(m,p) binomials and this implies that only m+1m+1 moments of GG are identifiable. Partial identification in discrete response models is discussed in more detail in [Koenker and Gu (2024)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib42) in the context of the [Kline and Walters (2021)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib41) model of employment discrimination.

In binomial mixture models with a large number of trials it is often convenient to transform to the Gaussian model as for example in the extensive literature on baseball batting averages, see e.g. [Gu and Koenker (2017a)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib25). In other settings it is more convenient to consider logistic models as for the Rasch model commonly used in educational testing or the Bradley-Terry model for rating participants in pairwise competition. See [Gu and Koenker (2022)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib27).

## 6. Empirical Bayes Methods for Panel Data

Longitudinal data poses many new challenges and opportunities for empirical Bayes methods. In this section we will reprise some prior work in [Gu and Koenker (2017b)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib26) on models of income dynamics and describe some extensions that broaden applicability of such models. The vast econometric literature on panel data methods has gradually embraced a wider variety of latent variable formulations designed to accommodate more general forms of heterogeneity. The quantile autoregression framework of [Arellano et al (2017)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib2) is notable in this regard. Empirical Bayes methods have a complementary role to play in this literature and also provide a flexible approach to modeling heterogeneity in panel data.

We will begin by considering a simple Gaussian location-scale model,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | yi​t=αi+θiui​t,t=1,⋯,mi,i=1,⋯,ny\_{it}=\\alpha\_{i}+\\sqrt{\\theta\_{i}}u\_{it},\\quad t=1,\\cdots,m\_{i},\\quad i=1,\\cdots,n |  |

with ui​t∼𝒩⁡(0,1)u\_{it}\\sim\\mathcal{N}(0,1). We need not interpret the tt subscript temporally, but it is frequently natural to do so. We will provisionally assume that αi∼Gα\\alpha\_{i}\\sim G\_{\\alpha} and θi∼Gθ\\theta\_{i}\\sim G\_{\\theta} are independent. We then have sufficient statistics:

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | y¯i\|αi,θi∼𝒩⁡(αi,θi/mi)\\bar{y}\_{i}\|\\alpha\_{i},\\theta\_{i}\\sim\\mathcal{N}(\\alpha\_{i},\\theta\_{i}/m\_{i}) |  |

and

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | Si\|ri,θi∼γ⁡(s\|ri,θi/ri),S\_{i}\|r\_{i},\\theta\_{i}\\sim\\gamma(s\|r\_{i},\\theta\_{i}/r\_{i}), |  |

|  |  |  |  |
|---|---|---|---|
|  | ℓ⁡(Gα,Gθ\|y)\\displaystyle\\ell(G\_{\\alpha},G\_{\\theta}\|y) | =K⁡(y)\\displaystyle=K(y) |  |
|  |  | +∑i=1nlog∫∫γ(Si\|ri,θ/ri)miϕ(mi(y¯i−αi)/θ)/θdGα(α)dGθ(θ).\\displaystyle+\\sum\_{i=1}^{n}\\log\\int\\int\\gamma(S\_{i}\|r\_{i},\\theta/r\_{i})\\sqrt{m\_{i}}\\phi(\\sqrt{m\_{i}}(\\bar{y}\_{i}-\\alpha\_{i})/\\sqrt{\\theta})/\\sqrt{\\theta}dG\_{\\alpha}(\\alpha)dG\_{\\theta}(\\theta). |  |

Since the scale component of the log likelihood is additively separable from the location component, we can solve for G^θ\\hat{G}\_{\\theta} in a preliminary step, and then solve for the G^α\\hat{G}\_{\\alpha} distribution. In fact, under the independent prior assumption, we can re-express the Gaussian component of the likelihood as Student-tt and thereby eliminate the dependence on θ\\theta in the NPMLE problem for estimating GαG\_{\\alpha}. An implementation of this estimation strategy is available with the function WTLVmix in the R package REBayes.

It is also possible to relax the independence assumption on the location and scale effects completely. In [Gu and Koenker (2017b)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib26) we use longitudinal data individuals from the Panel Study on Income Dynamics (PSID) to explore models of income dynamics with an arbitrary joint distribution of location and scale heterogeneity. We follow the sample selection of [Meghir and Pistaferri (2004)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib52) to focus on male head of households aged 25 - 55 with at least 9 years of consecutive earnings data. We further restrict our attention to those whose earning starts from age 25 onwards. This leaves us with 938 individuals for whom we observe at least the early portion of their life cycle earnings. Among the 938 individuals, 50% of those we observe have reported earnings of 15 years starting from age 25, The longest span of recorded earnings in the sample is 26 years.

The implementation employs the function WGLVmix from the REBayes package. In the income dynamics application we find an apparent negative dependence between the α\\alpha (location) and θ\\theta scale effects indicating that low “ability” individuals also tend to have higher income risk. In our prior work temporal dependence in the income process was specified as a simple AR(1) process whose coefficient, ρ\\rho was estimated by profile likelihood.

To make the AR(1) specification more explicit consider the model,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | yi​t=αi+βi​xi​t+vi​tvi​t=ρ​vi​t−1+θi​ϵi​t,ϵi​t∼𝒩⁡(0,σ2)\\begin{array}\[]{ll}y\_{it}&=\\alpha\_{i}+\\beta\_{i}x\_{it}+v\_{it}\\\\ v\_{it}&=\\rho v\_{it-1}+\\sqrt{\\theta\_{i}}\\epsilon\_{it},\\ \\ \\epsilon\_{it}\\sim\\mathcal{N}(0,\\sigma^{2})\\end{array} |  |

Assuming that initial conditions, yi​0y\_{i0}, are drawn from the stationary distribution 𝒩⁡(αi,θi/(1−ρ2))\\mathcal{N}(\\alpha\_{i},\\theta\_{i}/(1-\\rho^{2})) is a convenient option and yields an efficient estimator for ρ\\rho provided that the assumption holds. One could also consider less restrictive specifications for yi​0y\_{i0} at the cost of introducing additional parameters as in [Arellano (2003)](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03422v1#bib.bib1). However it seems simpler to consider Chamberlainian dependence of the latent effects on covariates, while trying to maintain a nonparametric perspective, a topic we defer to future research. As we will see, more complex short run dynamics can be introduced via state space representations and Kalman filtering formulations of the likelihood.

Fixing ρ\\rho and σ2\\sigma^{2}, and setting y\~i​t=yi​t−ρ​yi​t−1\\tilde{y}\_{it}=y\_{it}-\\rho y\_{it-1}, our model can be expressed as,

|  |  |  |
|---|---|---|
|  | y\~i​t=(1−ρ)​αi+θi​ϵi​t.\\tilde{y}\_{it}=(1-\\rho)\\alpha\_{i}+\\sqrt{\\theta\_{i}}\\epsilon\_{it}. |  |

And for Gaussian ϵi​t\\epsilon\_{it}, sufficient statistics for αi\\alpha\_{i} and θi\\theta\_{i} are respectively the sample mean and sample variance:
